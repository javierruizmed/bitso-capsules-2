The CSI has been volatile, oscillating between 54.3% and 90.0% across the period. Three patterns stand out.

First, survey volume is declining — from 137 responses in November to just 20 in April — a direct consequence of the form migration, which removed tickets and their associated surveys from the standard CS flow.

Second, the March dip to 54.3% is the most actionable signal in this dataset. It coincides with continued high friction in the account-opening flow and no structural improvement in the Proof of Address or PEP processes.

Third, the overall CSI range (54–74% excluding the low-sample April figure) suggests that even when CS resolves the issue, a meaningful portion of users leave the interaction unsatisfied — pointing to friction that sits upstream of CS, in the product itself.

⚠️ Data gap: A significant portion of Stocks experience data is unavailable because the temporary form used by Julia Gomes' team does not yet have CSAT surveys enabled. This is particularly relevant for account limits tickets.
03 Pain Points — Ticket Review

Beyond the quantitative data, a manual review of Stocks tickets surfaces a set of recurring friction patterns that the numbers alone don't fully capture.

🔴 Account opening blocked by documentation friction

The most common scenario: a user wants to start trading stocks but cannot complete the account setup because of issues with their documentation.

PEP Declaration errors: Three confirmed cases where users were blocked due to an incorrect PEP declaration — a critical blocker since it prevents account activation entirely #3186550 #3174692. A transition has already been made to handle these cases online by agents rather than escalating offline, which is a positive step. However, it is suggested that the current wording in the declaration flow be reviewed and refined to prevent accidental triggers — a confirmation step or "double-check" modal for high-impact selections would reduce these cases significantly.

Outdated Personal Address: At least one case where an outdated address prevented a user from accessing their stocks account #3178565. Users are not proactively informed their address must be current.

�DFE0 Stock limits process — a friction of its own

Users who want to trade above 25,000 MXN must go through a mandatory identity verification process. This is managed entirely by Julia Gomes and represents a separate — largely invisible — source of friction not fully captured in standard CS incoming data.

How it works today: Once a user requests a limit increase, Julia Gomes sends an initial invitation. The user must choose between:

• KOBRA (preferred by regulation): A compliance officer visits the home, photographs the property and ID documents, and collects a physical signature.
• DHL (fallback): A document is couriered to the user, signed, and returned. Only offered when KOBRA has no coverage or the user finds the home visit too invasive.

🔴 In April, 449 out of 528 tickets (85%) never progressed because users didn't respond to the initial invitation. This isn't disengagement — it reflects a process that users find too cumbersome to complete. Feedback confirms the home visit is perceived as invasive.

Why the process is this way: This is a regulatory requirement in Mexico — not a product choice. The physical verification visit is legally mandated for limit increases above the threshold. The infrastructure to digitize this exists, but regulatory approvals are still pending. A KOBRA API integration is being explored to let users schedule their visit directly from the Bitso app, removing Julia Gomes' manual outreach step.

What this means for CS: The process is entirely manual. Julia Gomes currently tracks each DHL waybill one by one — a workload that is not scalable and will need to be redistributed to the KYC team once the flow improves.

🔵 Warrants vs. Stocks — users can't tell the difference

Two tickets surfaced a recurring confusion: users cannot differentiate between regular stocks and Warrants #3200872 #3204174. Warrants are time-limited instruments that expire, but the app presents them alongside regular stocks without making this distinction clear.

When a Warrant expires and is no longer actionable, users who haven't used it don't understand what happened — they perceive it as losing an asset, which generates frustration and distrust in the platform. The instrument disappears or becomes inactive without the user having received a clear explanation of what a Warrant is and what its expiry terms mean.

Two improvements are suggested: either create a visual or structural separation between regular stocks and Warrants in the app interface, or ensure that Warrant listings include clear, prominent information about their nature, expiry date, and terms — so users can make informed decisions before they expire.
