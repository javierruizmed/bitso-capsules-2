Prepared by Javier Ruiz · Experience Senior Specialist · Bitso Customer Service  ·  #exp-retail-user-voice-capsule
This capsule covers Account Limits — a category where the user's ability to operate on Bitso is directly tied to completing identity verification successfully. When the verification journey breaks, users hit a hard stop that prevents them from accessing higher limits and transacting freely. This capsule takes a dual perspective: CS incoming data reveals what users experience, while KYC operational data reveals the systemic gaps that neither team can resolve alone. At the center of both: IDWall — Bitso's biometric verification provider — as a recurring point of failure.

06Operation Restitution — The Other Side of the IDWall Gap

The Operation Restitution case is the clearest example of what IDWall's verification gaps cost in practice. When the provider fails to detect manipulated biometrics — face swaps, static photo uploads, identity mismatches — it doesn't only block legitimate users. It also opens the door to the opposite problem: fraudulent accounts that pass verification they shouldn't. Both failures come from the same root cause: a provider whose detection model is not reliable enough for the volume and threat landscape Bitso operates in.

Three attack vectors — all exploiting IDWall gaps

🔴 M.O. 01 — Face swapping / Deepfake liveness (from March 20)

Attackers overlaid a real victim's face onto their own biometric video using real-time face-swap tools. IDWall's liveness detection auto-approved the check. Extreme case: the exact same liveness was approved across 52 different accounts.

🔴 M.O. 02 — CPF/biometric identity mismatch (from March 27)

Scammers registered any CPF in Hubble and validated a different person's biometrics in IDWall. No crossmatch existed between the two systems — account passed as fully verified with mismatched identity. 143 confirmed repeated-face static photo upload cases documented.

🟠 M.O. 03 — Delete-on-rejection + IRS timing (from April 7)

When IDWall rejected an attempt, attackers immediately deleted the account — erasing their trail. The operation was deliberately timed around the Brazilian IRS (Receita Federal) refund disbursement window.

07What Users Are Telling Us — And What Could Help

The data and verbatims in this capsule point to a consistent set of friction patterns. Below is a summary of what users experience, along with proposals for improvement.

📄 Documentation friction — users don't know what's expected

Users upload documents, get rejected, and receive no clear explanation. Many retry multiple times with the same result. Several verbatims show users submitting documents they believe are correct, only to find out later a specific field, format, or currency interpretation was wrong. The requirements are unclear, inconsistent across markets, and in some cases contradictory — one user noted the email said "gross income" while the agent asked for "net income."

💡
What could help: Clear, bullet-point requirements per market (MX, AR, BR). Visual examples of valid vs. invalid documents. Multi-document upload capability. Removal of "account statement" as a valid option. A message at the start of the process telling users what to expect and that more information may be requested.
🔍 IDWall reliability — a provider that needs closer oversight

Two IDWall incidents in 60 days (Feb 3 and Apr 1) directly drove the worst CSI readings of the period — and in February, every KYC sub-category hit its floor simultaneously. CS agents have no visibility when IDWall is degraded, so they ask users to retry without being able to explain why verification is failing.

💡
What could help: An internal alert for CS agents when IDWall is degraded, so they can set expectations proactively. A user-facing message that explains delays without exposing the provider. A review of IDWall's SLA and detection model performance as part of a formal vendor assessment.
🔄 Retry friction — users who are rejected get stuck

Multiple verbatims describe users who were rejected and then couldn't re-upload documents, or who had to compress files, convert formats, or submit via chat because the in-app flow blocked them. The system punishes the user for the rejection rather than enabling correction.

💡
What could help: Review the retry flow after a document rejection. Users should be able to re-upload without starting over, and should receive a specific explanation of what was wrong.
📊 Upgrade 1→2 — highest volume, worth a proportionality review

This is the single highest-volume breaking point in the period — peaking at 856 tickets in March. It's worth asking whether the documentation requirements at this tier are proportional to the limit being unlocked, and whether any can be simplified or deferred without compliance risk.
